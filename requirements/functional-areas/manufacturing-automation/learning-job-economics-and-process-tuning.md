# Learning-Job Economics and Process Tuning

## Problem

An early-stage shop needs real repetitions to expose process cost, quality,
setup, tooling, staffing, maintenance, scheduling, and automation problems.
Avoiding all low-margin work can leave capacity idle and prevent learning, but
uncontrolled discounting can consume cash, anchor customers to unsustainable
prices, hide true cost, and distract from better opportunities.

## Objective

Allow suitable early work to be intentionally classified as learning work at
break-even or a small approved loss when the repetitions are expected to
produce reusable process knowledge, improve automation, reduce future cost,
build trustworthy performance evidence, and create a credible path to
sustainable margin.

## Terminology

- **Learning job:** Commercial work intentionally accepted partly for
  structured operational learning.
- **Repetition:** A comparable unit, batch, setup, or recurring order that can
  be measured against prior work.
- **Normal price:** The price required to meet the approved long-term margin
  and risk targets.
- **Learning investment:** The approved difference between expected normal
  economics and the accepted learning-job economics.
- **Graduation:** The point at which the process and price move from learning
  treatment to normal commercial operation.

## Inputs

- normal and offered price;
- material and outside-process cost;
- setup, programming, fixture, and inspection effort;
- attended and unattended machine time;
- direct and indirect labor;
- consumables, tooling, energy, freight, and packaging;
- scrap, rework, and quality risk;
- payment terms and collection risk;
- available versus constrained capacity;
- customer and repeat-order potential;
- learning objectives;
- expected automation or process improvements;
- expected and maximum loss;
- cash reserve and monthly learning budget; and
- alternative uses of the capacity.

## Rules

1. A learning job must be labeled internally before acceptance.
2. The job must identify one or more measurable learning objectives.
3. Safety, quality, traceability, inspection, and customer requirements are not
   relaxed because the work has reduced margin.
4. The quote records normal price, accepted price, and learning investment
   separately, even if only the accepted price is shown to the customer.
5. Expected contribution, expected loss, maximum loss, and cash timing must be
   calculated before approval.
6. Work below the approved variable-cost or loss threshold requires explicit
   higher-level approval.
7. Learning work must not displace higher-value committed work without an
   explicit opportunity-cost decision.
8. Every repetition captures estimate versus actual for setup, cycle, attended
   labor, material, tooling, consumables, scrap, rework, inspection, delay, and
   delivery.
9. Repetitions must be sufficiently comparable before attributing improvement
   to learning or automation.
10. Post-job review records what changed, what was learned, what should be
    standardized, and the next improvement experiment.
11. Pricing must include an expiration, quantity limit, or graduation trigger
    so an introductory price does not silently become permanent.
12. The business must stop, reprice, redesign, automate, outsource, or reject
    the work when learning plateaus or the path to sustainable margin is not
    credible.
13. Employee feedback is part of the learning record; lower attended labor must
    not be achieved by creating unsafe workload, hidden unpaid effort, or
    unrecorded maintenance.
14. Customer confidentiality and prime-contractor relationships remain
    protected when the work is overflow subcontracting.

## Assumptions

- Early cost estimates have wide uncertainty.
- Some setup and process development benefits future jobs.
- Idle capacity has a cost but is not automatically worse than negative-margin
  work.
- A small number of well-selected repeat jobs is more useful than unrelated
  one-off work with no reusable learning.

## Constraints

- Maintain adequate working capital.
- Do not represent a learning process as proven.
- Do not accept work beyond demonstrated safety, quality, inspection, or
  regulatory capability.
- Do not use customer parts as uncontrolled experiments.
- Do not conceal known delivery or capability risk.
- Do not classify ordinary pricing mistakes as intentional learning
  investments after the fact.

## Failure Behavior

The system must block or escalate acceptance when:

- expected or maximum loss is missing;
- learning objectives are not measurable;
- cash exposure exceeds the approved budget;
- the job cannot meet safety or quality requirements;
- the customer requires unsupported certification or capability;
- the introductory terms have expired;
- prior repetitions show no credible improvement path;
- capacity is needed for higher-priority committed work; or
- estimate and actual data from prior repetitions is unavailable without an
  explained exception.

## Acceptance Criteria

1. Every learning job has a normal-price estimate, accepted price, expected
   contribution or loss, maximum loss, learning objective, approver, and
   graduation or stop condition.
2. Actual cost is recorded consistently across comparable repetitions.
3. A repetition report shows changes in cost, cycle, setup, attended labor,
   quality, delivery, and bottleneck state.
4. Improvement claims distinguish product-mix changes from process changes.
5. The system can show cumulative learning investment and cumulative realized
   savings by process, customer, and part family.
6. An expired introductory price cannot be reused without renewed approval.
7. Jobs that do not improve or cannot reach approved economics are visibly
   repriced, redesigned, outsourced, or stopped.
8. Customer-facing quality and delivery records remain separate from internal
   learning economics.

## Out of Scope

- Assuming all idle capacity should accept any positive-revenue work.
- Competing indefinitely on below-market price.
- Reducing safety, quality, inspection, employee pay, or legal compliance to
  manufacture at the learning price.
- Treating speculative future orders as guaranteed recovery of current losses.
