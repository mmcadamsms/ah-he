# Industrial Geography and Domestic Supply Chain Questions

## Purpose

This topic tracks where industrial capabilities, manufacturing capacity,
suppliers, and raw-material production are concentrated in the United States.

The answers may influence:

- where the business should initially operate;
- which customers and industries can be reached efficiently;
- access to skilled labor and engineering expertise;
- machine shops and specialized manufacturing partners;
- material availability, price, and lead time;
- freight cost and delivery speed;
- resilience to regional or supplier disruption; and
- future facility, warehouse, or supplier-network decisions.

These are open research questions, not conclusions about where the business
should locate.

## Questions Supplied in Conversation

### GEO-001 — Where are the industrial powerhouses in the USA?

**State:** Exploring

**First-pass answer:** The principal U.S. industrial systems are the Great
Lakes/Ohio Valley, Texas and the Gulf Coast, the Southeast auto-aerospace
corridor, Southern California and the Bay Area, the Northeast/Mid-Atlantic, and
the Pacific Northwest. They are powerful for different reasons; ranking them
requires a stated metric and detailed industry rather than reputation.

**Evidence:**
- [BEA GDP by state and industry](https://apps.bea.gov/itable/iTable.cfm?ReqID=70&step=1) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. It supports comparisons of
  current value added by industry and state.
- [County Business Patterns](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cbp/about.html) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. It derives establishment,
  employment, and payroll counts from the Census Business Register and
  administrative records.
- [Freight Analysis Framework](https://www.bts.gov/faf) — **Evidence class:**
  official measured logistics data. It maps commodity flows and freight
  gateways rather than relying on historical labels.

**Conflicting evidence:** High output, high establishment density, rapid
growth, and deep supplier capability produce different rankings.

**Confidence:** Medium-high on the broad systems; medium on order until a
business-specific metric is run.

**Business-specific validation:** Extract current BEA, CBP, QCEW, freight, and
utility data at state, metro, and county level and interview customers and
suppliers in finalists.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree with qualifications. The broad systems are right, but "industrial powerhouse" depends on metric and specialized hubs should not be hidden inside large region labels.

**Original wording:** "where are the industrial powerhouses in the USA?"

**Clarification needed from the eventual answer:**

- Define what qualifies as an industrial powerhouse.
- Distinguish industrial output from manufacturing output.
- Compare metropolitan areas, regions, states, and industrial corridors.
- Identify the dominant industries in each location.
- Include current scale, recent growth or decline, workforce, infrastructure,
  energy, logistics, research institutions, and supplier density.
- Separate historical reputation from present-day capability.

**Conflicting evidence:** Large plants may be vertically integrated or locked
to incumbents, while smaller clusters can outsource more high-mix work.

**Confidence:** Medium-high.

**Business-specific validation:** Map target NAICS buyers and relevant supplier
NAICS, then collect RFQs and supplier lead-time interviews in candidate metros.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree with qualifications. The manufacturing centers named are directionally correct, but total output alone overstates accessible custom-part demand for a new entrant.

**Original wording:** "where are the manufacturing powerhouses in the USA?"

- Define the measures used: employment, value added, establishment count,
  capital investment, exports, output, productivity, or supplier density.

- Break out relevant capabilities such as machining, metal fabrication,
  additive manufacturing, automotive, aerospace, medical devices, electronics,
  plastics, tooling, heat treatment, coatings, inspection, and assembly.
- Identify regions with the depth to support low-volume custom parts rather
  than only high-volume production.
- Describe labor availability, wage levels, lead times, logistics, utilities,
  incentives, and business climate without treating any one measure as
  decisive.

### GEO-003 — Where are manufacturers' raw materials made in the USA?

**State:** Exploring

**First-pass answer:** U.S. raw-material production is commodity-specific and
far more concentrated than distribution. Steel production is led by Indiana,
Ohio, Texas, and Pennsylvania; copper mining is dominated by Arizona; primary
aluminum operates at only a few smelters; titanium sponge had no U.S.
production in 2025. Service centers and converters make stock broadly
available, but that is not proof of U.S. melt or feedstock origin.

**Evidence:**
- [USGS 2026 iron and steel summary](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-iron-steel.pdf) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. It reports Indiana at an
  estimated 19% of 2025 raw steel, followed by Ohio, Texas, and Pennsylvania.
- [USGS 2026 aluminum summary](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-aluminum.pdf) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. Six primary smelters in five
  states operated in 2025 and net import reliance was estimated at 60%.
- [USGS 2026 copper summary](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-copper.pdf) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. Arizona supplied about 70% of
  domestic mine output; refined-copper net import reliance was 57%.
- [USGS 2026 titanium summary](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-titanium.pdf) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. No titanium sponge was produced
  domestically in 2025 and import reliance was 100%.

**Conflicting evidence:** “Made in USA” can describe conversion from imported
feedstock; recycled metal and semifinished imports further complicate origin.

**Confidence:** High for the cited metals; medium for plastics, composites,
wood, ceramics, and chemicals pending family-specific mapping.

**Business-specific validation:** Obtain mill certificates and distributor
quotes by alloy and stock form, including melt, conversion, inventory location,
minimum order, lead time, and substitute sources.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree. The commodity-specific mapping and domestic-origin cautions are empirically grounded and stronger than generic "made in USA" assumptions.

**Original wording:** "where are the raw materials that maufacturers use made in the USA?"

**Clarification needed from the eventual answer:**

- Distinguish extraction, primary production, refining, alloying, conversion,
  distribution, and recycling.
- Map important material families separately:
  - carbon, alloy, tool, and stainless steels;
  - aluminum;
  - copper and brass;
  - nickel, titanium, magnesium, and other specialty metals;
  - engineering plastics, resins, and composites;
  - industrial ceramics;
  - lumber and engineered wood;
  - elastomers, adhesives, coatings, and process chemicals.
- Identify where mills, smelters, refineries, resin plants, foundries, service
  centers, and major distributors are concentrated.
- Distinguish material described as made in the USA from material merely
  stocked, cut, converted, or distributed in the USA.
- Identify dependence on imported ore, feedstock, alloying elements, billet,
  slab, resin precursors, or finished stock.
- Include common stock forms relevant to custom manufacturing: plate, sheet,
  bar, tube, extrusion, castings, forgings, powder, filament, pellets, and
  composite prepreg.

property cost, congestion, and competition.

materials, and outside processes in three metros and test customer response

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree with qualifications. The candidate regions are credible, but the answer should soften any implication that a secondary metro usually wins because some primary metros justify higher cost with denser buyers and outside processes.

### GEO-008 — Which regions are resilient rather than merely inexpensive?

**State:** Exploring

**First-pass answer:** Resilience requires redundant customers, suppliers,
routes, utilities, and workforce plus acceptable hazard exposure; low cost
alone is not resilience. Candidate secondary metros in diversified industrial
regions may outperform single-employer towns and congested coastal hubs.

**Evidence:**
- [FEMA National Risk Index](https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps/products-tools/national-risk-index) —
  **Evidence class:** official modeled and historical risk data. It compares
  natural-hazard risk and expected loss at county and census-tract levels.
- [EIA electricity data](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/data.php) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured utility data. Reliability, prices, and
  utility characteristics can be compared using Form EIA-861 datasets.
- [Freight Analysis Framework](https://www.bts.gov/faf) — **Evidence class:**
  official measured logistics data. It exposes route and gateway dependence.

**Conflicting evidence:** Redundancy usually costs more; historically reliable
locations can still face correlated regional disasters or capacity shortages.

**Confidence:** High on framework; medium on location conclusions.

**Business-specific validation:** Conduct utility and insurer interviews,
supplier/customer concentration analysis, alternate-route trials, hazard
engineering, and continuity exercises for finalist sites.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree with qualifications. The resilience framework is right, but secondary metros are not automatically safer; correlated utility, supplier, or hazard exposure still has to be tested locally.

Consider supplier redundancy, energy reliability, water, transportation,
natural hazards, insurance, workforce depth, economic concentration, and
dependence on single employers or industries.

### GEO-010 — Which domestic-material claims matter to customers?

**State:** Exploring

**First-pass answer:** Provenance claims matter when incorporated into a
contract or regulated funding stream. “Buy American,” infrastructure “Buy
America,” and DFARS specialty-metal restrictions are different tests; ordinary
commercial buyers may instead require country of origin, mill traceability, or
recycled content.

**Evidence:**
- [FAR Part 25](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-25) — **Evidence class:**
  official regulation. It defines domestic end products and component-cost
  tests for federal procurement.
- [Build America, Buy America guidance](https://www.madeinamerica.gov/) —
  **Evidence class:** official regulatory guidance. Federally funded
  infrastructure has program-specific domestic-content requirements.
- [DFARS Part 225](https://www.acquisition.gov/dfars/part-225-foreign-acquisition) —
  **Evidence class:** official regulation. Defense sourcing includes distinct
  domestic restrictions and exceptions.

**Conflicting evidence:** U.S.-stocked, U.S.-machined, U.S.-manufactured, and
U.S.-melted are not equivalent; contract clauses and waivers can change the
test.

**Confidence:** High.

**Business-specific validation:** Have contracts counsel classify each target
market and obtain supplier certificates that preserve the exact required chain
of custody.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree. Provenance claims are contract- and regime-specific and should not be collapsed into one vague domestic-material label.

Determine when customers require US-melted, US-poured, US-extruded,
US-manufactured, Buy America, Buy American, DFARS-compliant, conflict-mineral,
recycled-content, or other provenance claims.

## Integrated Location Economics

### GEO-011 — What integrated location model should compare customer access, outbound shipping, materials, installed equipment, buildings, taxes, regulation, labor, utilities, and resilience?

**State:** Exploring

**First-pass answer:** Use a gated total-cost model: first eliminate sites that
fail zoning, building, utility, hazard, workforce, customer-access, or process
requirements; then compare ten-year cash flow and risk under several operating
scenarios. Do not collapse all factors into one untested weighted score.

**Evidence:**
- [BEA regional data tools](https://www.bea.gov/data-tools) — **Evidence class:**
  official measured economic data. They support regional industry and income
  comparisons.
- [Commodity Flow Survey](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/cfs.html) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured logistics data. It supports outbound
  and inbound freight assumptions.
- [FEMA National Risk Index](https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps/products-tools/national-risk-index) —
  **Evidence class:** official modeled risk data. It supports location-specific
  hazard screening.

**Conflicting evidence:** Weighted rankings can imply precision while hiding
nonlinear costs, hard constraints, and correlated risks.

**Confidence:** High.

**Business-specific validation:** Populate the model with property, utility,
labor, freight, tax, permit, insurance, equipment-installation, customer, and
supplier quotes for each finalist.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree. Gated screening before ten-year cash-flow comparison is the right model and is more defensible than an all-in weighted score.

### GEO-012 — Which location costs are one-time, fixed, variable, step-fixed, volume-dependent, or risk-contingent?

**State:** Exploring

**First-pass answer:** One-time costs include relocation, due diligence,
permits, buildout, rigging, and utility connection; fixed costs include rent,
base staffing, insurance, and taxes; variable costs include freight, energy,
consumables, and direct labor; step-fixed costs include added shifts, machines,
inspection, and supervisors; risk-contingent costs include delay, outage,
hazard, remediation, and permit change.

**Evidence:**
- [SBA calculate startup costs](https://www.sba.gov/business-guide/plan-your-business/calculate-your-startup-costs) —
  **Evidence class:** official guidance. It separates one-time from ongoing
  costs for planning.
- [EIA manufacturing energy consumption](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/manufacturing/) —
  **Evidence class:** official measured data. Manufacturing energy use varies
  materially by process and industry.

**Conflicting evidence:** Lease and labor may appear fixed but become step-
fixed or variable with shifts, overtime, expansion, and contract structure.

**Confidence:** High.

**Business-specific validation:** Assign each quoted line item a cost behavior,
volume driver, escalation, timing, and downside distribution in the location
model.

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree. The cost-behavior categories are correct, with the right warning that apparently fixed items can step or vary once shifts, volume, or expansion change.

**Business-specific validation:** Use separate scorecards and decision gates at

**Second-opinion review:** GPT-5.4 — Agree. Hierarchical geography screening is the right structure, and parcel-level disqualifiers can override attractive regional averages.
