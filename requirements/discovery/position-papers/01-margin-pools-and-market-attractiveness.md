# Position Paper 01 - Manufacturing Margin Pools and Market Attractiveness

## Status

- **Status:** Planned
- **Sequence:** 0
- **Decision:** Which manufacturing niches combine attractive margin,
  addressable market, growth, defensibility, and fit with the business?

## Core Question

Which areas within additive manufacturing, subtractive manufacturing,
fabrication, finishing, treatment, assembly, and creative metalwork combine
attractive, durable margin with a sufficiently large and reachable market?

The paper should compare possible areas of focus rather than assume ordinary
three-axis or five-axis milling is the best core business. The answer might
favor advanced machining, additive production, heat treatment, powder coating,
specialized finishing, urgent repair, tooling, high-end metal artwork, or a
combination. It might also conclude that a process with high apparent margin
has too little market, excessive capital, difficult qualification, or poor
strategic fit.

The opportunity must be evaluated across both dimensions:

1. **What process or service is sold?**
2. **Which end market, customer type, and demand environment buys it?**

Five-axis milling for aerospace, urgent machined replacements for oil and gas,
metal additive tooling for industrial production, and high-end metal artwork
may use overlapping capabilities while having very different market depth,
growth, qualification, competition, cyclicality, and saturation.

A process may have a high quoted margin while still being unattractive because
it requires expensive equipment, energy, permits, skilled labor, quality
systems, long qualification, high utilization, environmental controls, or
large downside risk. Heat treatment, coatings, specialized inspection, urgent
repair, and other apparently high-value services must be evaluated on complete
economics rather than selling price alone.

Compare:

- prototype and one-off machining;
- high-mix, low-volume machining;
- repeat production machining;
- three-axis milling;
- five-axis and other advanced milling;
- CNC turning, mill-turn, Swiss machining, grinding, and EDM;
- polymer additive manufacturing;
- metal additive manufacturing;
- additive tooling, fixtures, patterns, and bridge production;
- urgent replacement and downtime-critical work;
- overflow capacity for other shops;
- design plus manufacturing;
- reverse engineering and repair;
- fixtures, tooling, gauges, molds, dies, and workholding;
- laser-cut flat parts;
- forming and press-brake work;
- welded fabrication;
- assemblies and test;
- inspection, documentation, and source-inspection support;
- heat treatment;
- powder coating, painting, plating, anodizing, passivation, and other

- high-end architectural, artistic, sculptural, decorative, and customized
  metalwork;

- grinding, honing, lapping, EDM, and other specialist processes;

- vertically integrated combinations.

## Margin and Market Matrix

For every candidate niche, estimate:

- total addressable market;
- realistically serviceable market;
- attainable share for a new entrant;
- historical and expected growth;
- customer concentration;
- geographic concentration;
- local versus national reach;
- average order and customer value;
- repeat frequency;
- gross and contribution margin potential;
- capital and working-capital intensity;
- utilization needed for break-even;
- labor and skill intensity;
- qualification and sales-cycle length;
- regulatory, safety, environmental, and quality complexity;
- incumbent strength and market fragmentation;
- automation potential;
- ability to differentiate;
- cyclicality;
- scalability; and
- exit, resale, or strategic value.

### Process-by-end-market opportunity

Build a matrix that compares each plausible process or service against end
markets such as:

- oil, gas, refining, petrochemical, and pipeline;
- electric power, utilities, nuclear, renewables, and grid infrastructure;
- aerospace, defense, and space;
- automotive and transportation;
- industrial machinery and automation;
- semiconductor and electronics equipment;
- medical devices and laboratory equipment;
- agriculture, food processing, mining, and construction;
- marine, rail, and logistics;
- architectural, artistic, decorative, and luxury work;
- consumer and prosumer products;
- research institutions and universities;
- maintenance, repair, and operations; and
- other identified growth industries.

For each intersection, assess:

- annual customer spending accessible to the proposed business;
- number and concentration of customers;
- repeat demand and lifetime value;
- expected industry growth;
- installed supplier capacity;
- customer backlog and lead-time pressure;
- shortage of qualified suppliers;
- customer willingness to qualify new suppliers;
- how quickly a small entrant could saturate the reachable niche;
- room to add customers and grow share over time;
- qualification and certification barriers;
- incumbent relationships and switching costs;
- cyclicality and commodity exposure;
- geographic reach;
- achievable price and margin;
- capability and capital requirements; and
- fit with staged automation.

### Growth and capacity-shortage entry thesis

Test the hypothesis that entering a growing industry is easier when customer
demand is expanding faster than qualified supplier capacity. In that
environment, customers may be more willing to try a new supplier that is
responsive, qualified, transparent, and willing to perform work incumbents
cannot accept promptly.

The paper must not assume growth automatically creates an attractive opening.
It must determine:

- whether the growth creates outsourced work relevant to the planned
  capabilities;
- whether shortages are temporary or structural;
- whether customers will qualify a small entrant;
- whether excess demand is profitable or consists mainly of undesirable work;
- whether capital can be added before the shortage disappears;
- whether demand survives an industry downturn; and
- what evidence shows room to continue growing share after the first orders.

The paper must distinguish **market size** from **market capitalization**.
For private manufacturing niches, addressable market, growth, transaction
evidence, and attainable share are generally more useful than the market
capitalization of a few public companies. Public-company valuations and
transaction multiples may be used as secondary evidence when the underlying
business is genuinely comparable.

## Required Measures

Distinguish:

- revenue and pass-through revenue;
- gross and contribution margin;
- normalized EBITDA and operating profit;
- owner wages versus business profit;
- free cash flow;
- working capital;
- maintenance and replacement capital;
- return on invested capital;
- revenue and gross profit per employee, productive hour, machine, and square
  foot;
- break-even utilization;
- qualification and sales-cycle cost;
- scrap, rework, warranty, return, liability, and environmental cost;
- demand cyclicality and customer concentration; and
- downside value and recoverability of specialized assets.

## Required Analysis

Determine:

- what customers are actually paying for;
- what creates pricing power;
- whether the advantage is scarce capacity, engineering, urgency, quality,
  certification, integration, reliability, automation, data, or switching
  cost;
- how quickly competitors can copy the service;
- whether repeat work improves economics;
- how much margin depends on high utilization;
- whether the addressable market can support the required utilization;
- whether market growth and supplier-capacity shortages make entry easier;
- whether labor, energy, consumables, maintenance, or compliance scale with
  revenue;
- whether vertical integration creates value or merely internalizes a low-
  utilization process; and
- whether outsourcing preserves most customer value with less capital and
  risk.

## False-Margin Warnings

Do not treat the following as durable profit:

- unpaid or underpaid owner labor;
- deferred maintenance or capital replacement;
- backlog or emergency pricing that cannot persist;
- omitted facility, environmental, insurance, or quality cost;
- low equipment cost caused by unusual distressed purchases;
- pass-through material revenue;
- customer concentration;
- one unrepeatable large order;
- unsafe shortcuts;
- missing working-capital cost; or
- profit dependent on a single irreplaceable employee.

## Evidence

Use actual operating evidence where possible, supported by:

- Census Annual Survey of Manufactures and Economic Census;
- IRS Statistics of Income;
- BLS industry, wage, productivity, and employment data;
- NBER-CES manufacturing data;
- Fabricators and Manufacturers Association benchmarks;
- reputable commercial industry and transaction databases;
- public-company and acquisition evidence where comparable;
- interviews with owners, buyers, suppliers, and lenders; and
- actual quotes for equipment, facilities, labor, energy, insurance,
  consumables, permits, and outside processes.

## Required Position

The paper must:

- rank candidate niches using both economic margin and market attractiveness;
- present a margin-versus-market matrix;
- present a process-by-end-market opportunity matrix;
- identify high-margin but small or difficult niches;
- identify large markets with weak or commoditized margin;
- identify growing industries where demand exceeds qualified supplier capacity;
- estimate how quickly each reachable niche could saturate;
- identify paths for continuing to gain share after the initial entry;
- identify niches where automation could materially improve the combination;
- identify the most attractive initial wedge;
- identify the most attractive longer-term focus;
- identify capabilities worth coordinating but not initially owning;
- state the utilization and capital assumptions;
- identify the strongest contrary evidence;
- recommend market tests; and
- define the evidence required before capital is committed.
